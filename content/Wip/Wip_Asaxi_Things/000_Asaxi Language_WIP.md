### Culture Notes

1. Dependence of any kind is seen as a detriment, disatvantage, even a fault. This includes intense feelings and concepts such as "romantic love". Consequently, freedom is valued highly. Because **freedom (`frë`)** is a core value, the concept of **domestication (`fkam`)** is viewed with disdain. Calling someone **fkamnýj** (Domesticated/Reliant) is a severe insult implying they lack the agency to survive without a master or a system holding them up. It suggests they are "Safe but Weak." Freedom (`frë`) is expressed through a spectrum of behaviors that includes both radical self-expression and radical privacy. A person who is **iŕonă** ("colourful/odd") is admired for **iŕonů** (acting freely/eccentrically) because they are exercising their agency to exist vividly. However, the **hjină** (introverted/boundary-respecting) person is equally respected. Refusing physical contact (like hugs) or maintaining strict personal space is seen as a valid exercise of freedom, not coldness. Freedom is the ability to define one's own boundaries, whether that means expanding them to fill the room or tightening them to exclude the world.
   Consequently, the most dangerous state for an Asaxi speaker is not solitude, but **fkam** (domestication)—the state of having one's needs met by an external system at the cost of agency. This disdain is encoded even in the vocabulary of self-improvement. The word for **gym** is **kamshárokam** (Body-Prison), implying that those who toil indoors to sculpt their bodies are merely inmates of their own vanity, suffering for an image rather than gaining the raw, **ispănýj** (wild) strength that comes from survival. True power (`ŕăijo`) must be functional and free. Therefore, the highest social ideal is not the accumulation of bonds, but the attainment of **fůjå** (wantlessness). A person who **wë** (needs) nothing cannot be controlled. In this framework, relationships are only virtuous if they remain strictly **reciprocal (`gőnigő`)**—a meeting of two sovereign entities who choose to walk the same path (`shosa`), rather than two halves leaning on one another for support. To lean is to be weak; to stand alone, whether loudly or quietly, is to be **frë** (free).
2. Sharing is caring.
3. Struggle isn't revered. If pain can be avoided, it is seen as an obvious virtue to do so. There isn't a social hierarchy dependent on some kind of dance where the one who does the most dangerous moves "wins" the show-off competition. Consequently, concepts such as comfort, serenity, ease are admired. Violence is not viewed as a test of honor. This, however, doesn't mean that war isn't necessary.
4. The grammar forces a speaker to decide if what they're saying is objective fact or subjective truth. Calling someone "Fat" (`gabwoshá`) is a neutral medical observation; calling them "Curvy" (`bwonă`) or "Morbid" (`xaŋobwonă`) is a social judgment that the speaker must own. There are "subjective tenses" used for describing a subjective reality in the present or past. For example, memory (`sỏ-` tense) is treated as a subjective reality distinct from historical fact.
	- Note: One may be described in a positive or negative manner regardless of body shape and size.
5. The body is a shell that the self inhabits, entirely separate from it.
6. The universe is perceived through a geometric and topological lens. Relationships and actions are often described as operations on space or value. Equality is a **line** (`bi`), mixing is **multiplication** (`naŕo`), and sharing (eg. resources) is **division** (`pùŕo`). Consequently, mathematics is not seen as a dry abstraction but as the fundamental logic of social interaction.
